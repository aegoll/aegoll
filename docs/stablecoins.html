Stablecoins and irreversibility

An agent that pays a human-scale invoice by card can be rescued afterwards. An agent that pays $40,000 in USDC to the wrong address cannot. There is no chargeback, no issuer to call, and no reversal — which is why a governance layer for autonomous spending has to decide before the payment rather than review after it.

Why do AI agents pay in stablecoins? Because the alternatives assume a human. A card needs a cardholder, a bank transfer needs banking hours, and both assume someone who can be phoned. A stablecoin transfer settles in seconds, costs a fraction of a cent, works at machine scale and needs no counterparty relationship — and it is final the moment it settles.

The rail: HTTP 402, USDC, six decimals

x402 revives the HTTP status code nobody used — 402 Payment Required — as a machine-to-machine payment handshake. A client requests a resource, the server answers 402 with a price and an address, the client pays and retries with proof. No account, no API key negotiation, no invoice.

Irreversibility is the whole argument

Human payment systems are built on the assumption that mistakes get undone. That assumption is load-bearing and it is absent here.

Reversal window120 days typicalhours, sometimesnone

Wrong recipientrecoverablesometimes recoverablegone
Practical control pointafterduringbefore

Every control in a card network is detective — it finds the problem and then unwinds it. Detection is worth having, and on this rail it buys you a description of a loss rather than a recovery. So the useful controls move forward in time, and a limit that is only checked in a monthly report is not a limit at all.

This cuts against the intuition that crypto rails need less governance because they are permissionless. Permissionless means nobody will stop a mistake for you. Finality means nobody can undo one. Those two properties together are the reason an autonomous agent with a wallet needs a spend decision on every action, not a spending report every month.

Why money is an integer here, and never a float

USDC has six decimals, so $2.50 is exactly 2500000 atomic units. That number is exact; 2.50 as a binary float is not, and the error compounds over a run of micropayments. 

>>> 0.1 + 0.2
0.30000000000000004          # a governance layer that accumulates here is wrong

>>> from tesoro.domain import usd_to_atomic
>>> usd_to_atomic("0.1") + usd_to_atomic("0.2")
300000                        # exactly $0.30

Passing a float to tesoro raises TypeError at the boundary rather than being tolerated, and the rounding direction is pinned so that a fraction of an atomic unit never rounds in the spender's favour. $0.0000001 becomes 0, not 1: rounding that favours the spender is a slow leak rather than an error, and a leak nobody reconciles is a leak nobody finds.

This is one of four hard invariants and it exists because of the rail, not because of taste. A float-denominated budget on an irreversible rail is a governance layer that will eventually approve something it believed was smaller.

Two channels, two currencies, one agent

An agent that pays for things also pays to think, and the two are not the same money.

They never share an envelope. Spending them from one budget would let an expensive afternoon of reasoning consume the money meant for purchases, and it would make the report meaningless — two currencies summed into one number is not a total, it is a coincidence.

Authorise, then settle — and why the envelope moves on the second

A payment on this rail has two moments, and conflating them is how a budget drifts:

  • Authorisation — the decision, before anything moves. This is where every control runs, where a verdict is produced, and where the record of which control decided is written.
  • Settlement — what actually happened on chain. It may differ from what was authorised, it may fail, and it may never occur because the agent abandoned the task.

Cumulative envelopes consume on settlement, not authorisation. An agent that asks about a $40 purchase and then abandons it has spent nothing, and a budget that had already deducted the $40 would refuse a later legitimate purchase for money that was never sent. The reverse error is worse: settling more than was authorised must consume the larger amount, and a version of this layer that consumed only the authorised figure let $5.00 settle against a $0.05 authorisation while recording $0.05 as spent.

What Tesoro does not touch

Worth being exact, because "agent payments" invites the assumption that this software moves money. It does not.

  • No custody. Tesoro holds no funds and no wallet. It decides; something else pays.
  • No keys. A key is never stored, logged or journalled, and a masked display path is the only way one is ever shown.
  • No chain interaction. No RPC, no signing, no broadcasting. It never touches a node, which is also why a decision costs microseconds rather than a block time.
  • No address validation. Whether an address is the right one is outside what a spend control can know. Tesoro governs how much, to whom by identifier, and for what declared purpose — not whether a counterparty is who it claims.
  • No AML screening engine. There is a schema for an assessment and no engine behind it: no list, no matching, no jurisdiction model. No regulatory compliance is claimed or sought.

Does a per-payment cap solve this?

No, and the reason is arithmetic rather than opinion. A cap of $10 per payment permits an unbounded number of $9.99 payments, and on a rail where each one costs a fraction of a cent to send there is nothing to make that irrational. Bounding the total needs a cumulative envelope over a window; bounding the number of actions needs a count envelope, which is a different quantity again.

The four kinds of AEGL page sets out what each level of control can and cannot express, and why a per-payment cap is honestly enough for some deployments and structurally insufficient for others.

What about cards, AP2 or bank rails?

The governance model is deliberately rail-neutral: envelopes, verdicts, attribution and evidence say nothing about USDC. x402 is the first binding because it is where autonomous machine payments are actually happening, and a card or AP2 binding changes the adapter rather than the controls.

What does change per rail is how much the reversibility column above lets you relax. On a card, a missed control is a dispute. Here it is a transfer. The controls in a policy pack are the same either way; the cost of getting them wrong is not.
